\section{Ablation Studies}

This section analyzes the contribution of different components of the proposed method under the official CUB-200-2011 train/test protocol. The ablation studies are organized according to the research questions defined in Chapter~1. The fusion-design ablation addresses RQ2 and RQ3 by separating the effects of species-level fusion, auxiliary attribute supervision, and attribute-query cross-attention. The attribute selection and subset-size ablations address RQ4 by comparing HSDAS with FullAttr-312, Sibling-IVAS-188, Random-199, and different HSDAS subset sizes. The diversity-loss ablation addresses RQ5 by varying \(\lambda_{div}\). The random attribute control further analyzes whether HSDAS-199 improves over group-matched random masks or simply benefits from using fewer attributes.
Unless otherwise stated, attribute-guided variants use HSDAS-199, species-level fusion, \(\lambda_{attr}=0.5\), and \(\lambda_{div}=0.5\). For direct-fusion baselines, the attribute-query cross-attention module is removed and \(\lambda_{div}=0\), because the direct-fusion model does not produce attribute-query cross-attention maps.

The direct-fusion baselines provide the control requested by RQ3. Overall, this ablation shows that the final improvement is not caused by a single factor. Direct species-level fusion can already improve over the image-only baseline, suggesting that additional species-level transformation capacity contributes to the gain. Auxiliary attribute supervision provides further semantic guidance, and the full cross-attention-based model with diversity regularization gives the strongest fine-grained classification result.





\subsection{Inference-Time Attribute Summary Ablation}

The previous ablations compare different trained model variants. To further examine whether the trained species classifier actually uses the attribute-oriented representation, this thesis conducts an inference-time ablation on the attribute summary vector. In this experiment, the model is trained normally using the full proposed setting, and only the attribute summary vector is modified during evaluation. No retraining is performed.

This analysis considers three diagnostic interventions. First, in the \textit{zero-summary} setting, the attribute summary vector is replaced with a zero vector before the fusion MLP. This tests whether the trained species classifier depends on the attribute summary. Second, in the \textit{shuffled-summary} setting, attribute summary vectors are shuffled across images within the same mini-batch. This preserves the general distribution of attribute summaries but breaks the correspondence between the input image and its attribute-oriented representation. Third, in the \textit{CLS-only} setting, the attribute summary vector and fusion MLP are bypassed, and the species class token is directly passed to the species classifier. This last setting is a strong diagnostic intervention rather than a fair baseline, because the species classifier was trained on fused species features rather than on the raw species class token.

Table~\ref{tab:inference_attr_summary_ablation} shows that the attribute summary vector has a clear effect on species-level prediction. When the attribute summary is replaced with a zero vector, species accuracy decreases from 85.83\% to 82.33\%, and FPA decreases from 84.36\% to 81.03\%. TICE also increases from 3.62\% to 6.28\%. This indicates that the trained species classifier does not ignore the attribute-oriented representation.

The shuffled-summary setting causes a much larger degradation. Species accuracy decreases to 72.32\%, FPA decreases to 71.23\%, and TICE increases to 15.91\%. Since the shuffled setting preserves the existence and general distribution of attribute summary vectors but mismatches them across images, this result suggests that the attribute summary is image-conditioned and must correspond to the current input image to support species prediction. In other words, the attribute summary does not simply act as a constant bias term; it carries image-dependent information used by the species classifier.

Family and order accuracies remain almost unchanged across the zero-summary and shuffled-summary settings. This is expected because the attribute-guided fusion is applied only to the species-level branch. Family and order predictions are still produced from the higher-level H-CAST stages and are not directly fused with the attribute summary vector. Similarly, attribute prediction accuracy remains almost unchanged because the attribute heads are computed from attribute-oriented features before the attribute summary is modified.

The CLS-only setting leads to a near collapse of species accuracy. This result should be interpreted carefully. The CLS-only setting bypasses both the attribute summary and the fusion MLP, and passes the raw species class token directly to a classifier that was trained on fused species features. Therefore, this setting introduces a severe distribution shift and should not be interpreted as an image-only H-CAST baseline. Instead, it is reported as a diagnostic stress test showing that the trained species head is highly dependent on the fused representation produced by the proposed species-level branch.

Overall, this inference-time ablation provides additional evidence that the performance gain of the proposed model is related to the attribute-oriented representation used in the species branch. Together with the fusion-design ablation, it supports the interpretation that attribute-guided learning improves H-CAST primarily by modifying the species-level representation and decision boundary, rather than by producing visually obvious attention localization patterns.

Attribute prediction accuracy is reported only as a diagnostic metric. Random-199 obtains the highest attribute prediction accuracy, but this does not translate into the best species accuracy or wAP. This shows that attribute prediction accuracy and final classification performance are not perfectly correlated. A random subset may contain attribute states that are easier to predict, while HSDAS selects attributes according to hard-sibling discrimination.

The results in Table~\ref{tab:diversity_loss_ablation} suggest that the diversity loss has a non-monotonic effect. The main setting with \(\lambda_{div}=0.5\) achieves the best FPA and species accuracy, suggesting that a moderate amount of diversity regularization can help fine-grained species discrimination. However, weaker diversity \((\lambda_{div}=0.1)\) and stronger diversity \((\lambda_{div}=1.0)\) do not improve species accuracy over the no-diversity setting. Therefore, the results do not support the claim that simply increasing diversity regularization always improves performance.

The no-diversity setting obtains the lowest TICE. This indicates that the model can produce more taxonomically consistent paths when the diversity regularizer is removed. In contrast, the best species accuracy is obtained with \(\lambda_{div}=0.5\), which has higher TICE. This suggests a trade-off between species-level discrimination and taxonomy consistency.

One possible explanation is that the diversity loss encourages less redundant internal attention patterns among broad attribute groups, which may improve species prediction. However, because family and order predictions are not directly fused with attribute features, changes in species prediction may not always be matched by corresponding changes in the coarser predictions. This can increase path inconsistency even when species accuracy improves.

The result with \(\lambda_{div}=1.0\) suggests that too much diversity regularization may become harmful. A strong diversity constraint may over-regularize the internal attribute-group representations, especially when only a few cues are useful for a particular image. Therefore, the diversity loss should be treated as a regularizer that requires careful weighting rather than a universally beneficial component.









\subsection{Random Attribute Control}

This control further supports RQ4 by testing whether the performance of HSDAS-199 comes from the hard-sibling-aware selection criterion or from using a compact group-matched attribute subset with the same number of retained states per group. In this thesis, Random-199 is constructed as a group-matched random control. It does not sample 199 attributes uniformly from the full 312-dimensional attribute space. Instead, for each semantic attribute group, it randomly samples the same number of attribute states as the HSDAS-199 mask. Therefore, Random-199 preserves the same group-level structure and active-group coverage as HSDAS-199, while removing the hard-sibling-aware selection criterion.
Mean and standard deviation are reported only for Random-199 because this experiment explicitly studies the variability caused by random attribute-mask sampling. Each random seed corresponds to a different group-matched attribute subset. The other settings, such as HSDAS-199, FullAttr-312, Sibling-IVAS-188, and the fusion or diversity variants, use fixed model configurations and fixed attribute masks; due to computational cost, they are trained once under the official CUB train/test protocol. Therefore, their results are reported as single-run values. A full multi-seed evaluation of all model variants would provide a stronger estimate of training variability, but it is outside the computational scope of this thesis.

The Random-199 control in Table~\ref{tab:random_control} shows that group-matched random subsets can be competitive. This is expected because Random-199 keeps the same number of attribute states in each semantic group as HSDAS-199. As a result, it still preserves broad coverage over color, shape, pattern, and part-related attribute groups. Therefore, this control is stronger than a purely uniform random subset over all 312 attributes, where the group distribution could become unbalanced.

Random-199 seed 2 obtains slightly higher FPA than HSDAS-199. However, this does not contradict the main conclusion of this thesis. FPA depends on the joint correctness of species, family, and order predictions. HSDAS-199 achieves higher species accuracy than seed 2, improving from 85.66\% to 85.83\%, and also achieves higher wAP, improving from 87.89\% to 88.00\%. These metrics are more directly related to fine-grained species discrimination, which is the main goal of the proposed attribute selection strategy. In contrast, seed 2 obtains higher family accuracy and lower TICE, which can lead to slightly better full-path correctness in this particular run. The FPA difference is only 0.04 percentage points, corresponding to roughly two test images. Therefore, this result is better interpreted as random variation among strong group-matched attribute subsets rather than evidence that random selection is superior.

Across the three random seeds, HSDAS-199 achieves higher species accuracy and wAP than every Random-199 run. It also achieves higher FPA than the Random-199 mean. This suggests that hard-sibling-aware selection provides a useful bias toward attributes that are helpful for species-level recognition. At the same time, the advantage is moderate rather than large. Since Random-199 keeps 199 out of 312 attributes and preserves the same group-level coverage as HSDAS-199, it can still contain many useful attribute states.

The TICE results also show that HSDAS-199 should not be claimed to dominate random selection in every metric. All three Random-199 seeds obtain lower TICE than HSDAS-199. This means that the random subsets can produce more taxonomically consistent prediction paths in some runs, even though they do not achieve the best species accuracy or wAP. This observation is consistent with the broader trend in the ablation studies: improving species-level discrimination does not always lead to the lowest taxonomy inconsistency.

The attribute accuracy of Random-199 is also higher than that of HSDAS-199. This result should be interpreted carefully. Attribute prediction accuracy measures how well the auxiliary heads predict the selected attribute states, but it does not directly measure whether those attributes are useful for species classification. Random masks may include more frequent or easier-to-predict attribute states, allowing the auxiliary attribute heads to achieve higher accuracy. In contrast, HSDAS selects attributes according to hard-sibling discriminativeness, which may produce harder attribute targets but more species-relevant supervision. This explains why HSDAS-199 has lower attribute prediction accuracy than Random-199 but higher species accuracy and wAP.

Overall, the random control shows that attribute subset size and group-level coverage already provide a strong baseline. HSDAS-199 should therefore not be interpreted as outperforming random selection in every metric. Its advantage is more specific: it provides a targeted and interpretable hard-sibling-aware selection strategy that achieves higher species accuracy and wAP than all three group-matched random subsets, while also achieving higher FPA than the Random-199 mean.
